\section{Dataset Generation}\label{Sec:data_gen}
In order to rigorously test the applicability of \glspl{gan} several new datasets had to be generated. Training deep learning algorithms for 3D geomodelling requires a substantial volume of high-fidelity training data. The new datasets were generated using FLUMY~\parencite{martin-hal-01313232}, a process-based stochastic modelling software designed to reproduce meandering channelized reservoirs. Meandering channelized reservoirs are accurately replicated because FLUMY simulates facies, relative grain size, and depositional time for each voxel in a 3D grid based on specified hydraulic and sedimentological rules. Geologically realistic features are produced without requiring excessive computational overhead, making the software not very computationally intensive thus making it possible to generate a large number of models in a relatively short amount of time.  

The FLUMY python distribution is based on Non-Expert User Settings (NEXUS) that only allow three customizable parameters: 1) Maximum Channel Depth, 2) Maximum Sand Body Extension, and 3) Net to Gross. Based on these three customizable inputs, the rest of the parameters as provided in the \href{https://flumy.minesparis.psl.eu/download/}{user guide} is estimated automatically. Although the software is specifically designed to simulate fluvial meandering rivers, its underlying process-based parameters can be intentionally adapted to mimic alternative depositional domains. Mimicking these alternative depositional domains allows the software to effectively capture the complex deltaic architectures hypothesized for the Delft Sandstone reservoir.

The hypothesized depositional environments are split across three discrete suites, Ranging from a Lower plain to Upper plain deltaic characteristics in terms of increasing channel depth and wider channel bodies. Beyond these specific NEXUS constraints, all generated models share a uniform reservoir scale with a total grid resolution fixed at 128×128×32 voxels. In order to allow for well placement in the samples, the final dimension should encompass an area of at least 2000x2000m as the \textbf{DEL-GT-01} and \textbf{DEL-GT-02-S2} have an 1500m spacing between them. Establishing this specific grid resolution combined with a standard voxel dimension of 20 m×20 m×1 m means that the total simulated domain securely covers a volume of 2560 m×2560 m×32 m. Every single voxel within this acute 32~m vertical interval is ultimately encoded with 1 of the 14 distinct facies (see \ref{tab:facies_config_overview}) available in FLUMY, of which not all will be present in the dataset samples due to the input parameter settings.

\subsection{Sample preprocessing}
To comprehensively assess the capabilities of \glspl{gan}, the synthetic training datasets are prepared at two distinct categorical resolutions. These two distinct resolutions consist of a high-fidelity 9-facies dataset and an upscaled 3-facies dataset. The high-fidelity 9-facies dataset preserves the detailed depositional environments generated by FLUMY, serving as a baseline to evaluate the model's pure generative capacity and its ability to replicate highly complex spatial distributions. While evaluating this generative capacity is crucial, practical field application requires conditioning the model to actual subsurface observations. These actual subsurface observations, derived from the cored section of the \textbf{DEL-GT-01} well, are strictly classified into three primary facies associations (Section \ref{subsec:geology_DGR}). Because the hard well data only resolves to three distinct associations, the original FLUMY depositional environments must be grouped accordingly to create the upscaled 3-facies dataset. This grouping process aggregates the detailed environments into broader categories, as detailed in Table \ref{tab:3_facies_grouping}.

Beyond this grouping process, the categorical nature of both the 3-facies and 9-facies datasets necessitates a final preprocessing step known as one-hot encoding~\parencite{goodfellow-et-al-2016}. One-hot encoding is critical because standard neural networks process continuous numerical gradients and might otherwise incorrectly interpret integer facies labels as having an ordinal, mathematical relationship. This ordinal misinterpretation is prevented by transforming the original 3D volume into an expanded 4D tensor, where each facies class is assigned its own dedicated spatial channel. Each spatial channel indicates the presence or absence of a specific facies using values of 1 and -1, a bipolar scaling choice specifically optimized for the activation functions commonly used in GAN architectures~\parencite{bhavsar2024stable}.

\section{Fluvgan Framework}\label{Subsec:Fluvgan}
\subsection{Model Architecture}\label{Subsec:Model_architecture}
The \gls{fluvgan} model, introduced by~\textcite{rongier2025afluvgan}, relies on an optimized DCGAN architecture for 3D fluvial facies modelling. This architecture adapts the BigGAN framework~\parencite{brock2018large} to manage the complex connectivity and high resolution necessary for realistic reservoir models. These models originally generated continuous values, which differs from this project's need for categorical facies data. Categorical facies data is therefore processed using the previously described one-hot encoding strategy, which assigns each facies to a separate output channel. These separate output channels dictate the final depth of the generator's tensor, represented by the variable $C$, where $C = 3$ or $C = 9$ depending on the selected experimental track. 

This dynamic channel depth is accommodated by an architecture comprising a Generator ($\mathbf{G}$) and a Discriminator ($\mathbf{D}$) built with 3D Residual blocks. These blocks enable the generator to transform a 100-dimensional latent vector $\mathbf{z}$ into a $C \times 32 \times 128 \times 128$ volume. This volume begins as a $512 \times 4 \times 4 \times 4$ feature map, created via a 3D transposed convolution, and is then expanded by five residual upscaling blocks. These upscaling blocks accommodate grid anisotropy by doubling spatial dimensions up to 128, while limiting depth doubling to the first three blocks to reach 32. This final spatial resolution is processed through a Softmax activation, ensuring the $C$ facies channels sum to exactly one per voxel. Every generated voxel is then evaluated by the discriminator, acting as a binary classifier to reduce the $C \times 32 \times 128 \times 128$ input to a single scalar logit. This logit is produced through five residual downscaling blocks that iteratively reduce spatial resolution while increasing the channel count to 512. This 512-channel discriminator, alongside the generator, achieves training stability by utilizing spectral normalization and LeakyReLU activations. Training stability is essential for managing the model's computational scale, which involves roughly 11.6 million trainable parameters for the generator and 2.0 million for the discriminator.

\subsection{Model Inversion}
While the 9-facies models are utilized to evaluate unconstrained generative performance, the 3-facies models are deployed to test practical conditioning against the \textbf{DEL-GT-01} well data. To successfully condition this 3-facies generative model to real-world observations, the overall inversion workflow relies on two primary mechanisms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Latent Space Optimization:} An iterative search to find the optimal random noise vector $\mathbf{z}^*$ that best approximates the well data.
    \item \textbf{Pivotal Tuning:} A targeted fine-tuning of the generator's mathematical weights to perfectly lock the generated facies to the hard conditioning data.
\end{itemize}

This two-step inversion process begins with latent space optimization. 
Latent space optimization freezes the internal weights of the pre-trained generator and utilizes gradient descent to iteratively search for an optimal latent vector $\mathbf{z}^*$. 
This optimal vector is found by minimizing a categorical cross-entropy loss between the generated facies at the exact spatial coordinates of the well and the true geological observations. 
These geological observations anchor the optimization, mathematically defined as:
$$ \mathbf{z}^* = \arg\min_{\mathbf{z}} \mathcal{L}(G(\mathbf{z})_{\text{well}}, \mathbf{y}_{\text{well}}) $$

This minimization yields an optimized vector $\mathbf{z}^*$ that often provides a close structural approximation, but frequently fails to achieve a perfect 100\% match due to the inherent mathematical boundaries of the learned latent space. 
These latent space boundaries are overcome by transitioning to the second optimization phase, known as Pivotal Tuning. 
Pivotal Tuning uses $\mathbf{z}^*$ as a static anchor, temporarily unfreezing the generator's internal weights $\theta$ to make microscopic adjustments. 
These weight adjustments forcefully push the localized output to match the exact well data, while a strict regularization term prevents the collapse of the broader geological realism, expressed as:
$$ \theta^* = \arg\min_{\theta} \mathcal{L}(G_\theta(\mathbf{z}^*)_{\text{well}}, \mathbf{y}_{\text{well}}) + \lambda ||\theta - \theta_0|| $$


\section{Evaluation Metrics}\label{Sec:eval}
Validating the outputs generated by the network requires a comprehensive suite of mathematical metrics designed to evaluate local precision, global realism, and overall ensemble diversity:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) \& Wasserstein Loss:} Evaluates pixel-wise classification accuracy and the preservation of global geological structures.
    \item \textbf{Normalized \& Jensen-Shannon Entropy:} Measures pixel-wise spatial uncertainty and total ensemble sample diversity.
    \item \textbf{Macro F1-Score:} Quantifies the localized mismatch at the wellbore while accounting for severe facies imbalance.
\end{itemize}

Evaluating the generated realizations begins by analyzing the foundational mathematical metrics, specifically Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) and Wasserstein Loss. 
BCE is deployed because the generator processes one-hot encoded facies, effectively turning the spatial generation into a multi-channel binary classification task. 
This classification task relies on BCE to penalize pixel-wise deviations between the generated probabilities ($\hat{y}_i$) and the true categorical labels ($y_i$), defined mathematically across $N$ voxels as:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{BCE} = - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left[ y_i \log(\hat{y}_i) + (1 - y_i) \log(1 - \hat{y}_i) \right] $$

While this equation effectively measures local, pixel-to-pixel accuracy, it structurally fails to capture the broader spatial connectivity of complex geological architectures. 
Complex geological architectures and global multi-point statistics are instead evaluated using the Wasserstein Loss, often implemented via the Mean Sliced Wasserstein Distance (MSWD). 
Wasserstein Loss calculates the optimal transport cost (Earth Mover's Distance) between the true ($P_r$) and generated ($P_g$) probability distributions, allowing the network to converge on highly realistic, non-linear structures even when exact pixel overlap is minimal. 
This structural distance is expressed as:

$$ \mathcal{W}(P_r, P_g) = \inf_{\gamma \sim \Pi(P_r, P_g)} \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim \gamma} [\|x - y\|] $$

Beyond evaluating the structural realism of a single generated model, it is critical to quantify the spatial uncertainty across the entire generated ensemble. 
This ensemble uncertainty is quantified using entropy metrics, beginning with Normalized Shannon's Entropy. 
Normalized Shannon's Entropy evaluates the pixel-wise conflict across multiple stochastic realizations, scaling the standard entropy equation by the number of classes ($k$) to strictly bound the metric:

$$ H_{norm}(X) = -\frac{1}{\log_2(k)} \sum_{i=1}^{k}P(x_i)\log_2 P(x_i) $$

This $[0, 1]$ bounding scale allows to visually map exact regions of spatial uncertainty, highlighting areas where the ensemble highly agrees versus areas of deep geological conflict. 
This 3D grid of spatial conflict is ultimately aggregated into a single definitive scalar using Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence. 
JS divergence measures the total variation and stochastic spread between the probability distributions of the ensemble, acting as a final quantitative measure of sample diversity:

$$ D_{JS}(P \parallel Q) = \frac{1}{2} D_{KL}(P \parallel M) + \frac{1}{2} D_{KL}(Q \parallel M) $$

where $M = \frac{1}{2}(P + Q)$ and $D_{KL}$ represents the standard Kullback-Leibler divergence.

While JS divergence defines the overall ensemble spread, each individual realization within that ensemble must still strictly honor the physical reality at the wellbore. 
This physical reality at the wellbore acts as a strict spatial constraint, the localized mismatch of which is calculated using the Macro F1-score. 
The Macro F1-score calculates the unweighted average of the harmonic mean of precision and recall across all $C$ individual facies classes:

$$ F1_{macro} = \frac{1}{C} \sum_{c=1}^{C} \frac{2 \cdot \text{Precision}_c \cdot \text{Recall}_c}{\text{Precision}_c + \text{Recall}_c} $$

Evaluating every facies class independently provides a highly robust performance metric that heavily penalizes the model if it ignores underrepresented minority classes. These underrepresented minority classes, such as the thin crevasse splays (FA2), would otherwise be completely overshadowed by the massive channel sand deposits (FA1) if standard statistical accuracy was deployed to assess the well conditioning.
