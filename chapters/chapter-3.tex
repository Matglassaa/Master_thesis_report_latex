\section{Dataset Generation}\label{Sec:data_gen}
In order to rigorously test the applicability of \glspl{gan} several new datasets had to be generated. Training deep learning algorithms for 3D geomodelling requires a substantial volume of high-fidelity training data. The new datasets were generated using FLUMY~\parencite{martin-hal-01313232}, a process-based stochastic modelling software designed to reproduce meandering channelized reservoirs. Based on sedimentological rules, the software reproduces facies type, grain size and deposition time for each voxel in th 3D volume.

\subsection{Sample preprocessing}
To comprehensively assess the capabilities of \glspl{gan}, the synthetic training datasets are prepared at two distinct categorical resolutions. These two distinct resolutions consist of a high-fidelity 9-facies dataset and an upscaled 3-facies dataset. The high-fidelity 9-facies dataset preserves the detailed depositional environments generated by FLUMY, serving as a baseline to evaluate the model's pure generative capacity and its ability to replicate highly complex spatial distributions. While evaluating this generative capacity is crucial, practical field application requires conditioning the model to actual subsurface observations. These actual subsurface observations, derived from the cored section of the \textbf{DEL-GT-01} well, are strictly classified into three primary facies associations (Section \ref{subsec:geology_DGR}). Because the hard well data only resolves to three distinct associations, the original FLUMY depositional environments must be grouped accordingly to create the upscaled 3-facies dataset. Beyond this grouping process, the categorical nature of both the 3-facies and 9-facies datasets necessitates a final preprocessing step known as one-hot encoding~\parencite{goodfellow-et-al-2016}. One-hot encoding is critical because standard neural networks process continuous numerical gradients and might otherwise incorrectly interpret integer facies labels as having an ordinal, mathematical relationship. This ordinal misinterpretation is prevented by transforming the original 3D volume into an expanded 4D tensor, where each facies class is assigned its own dedicated spatial channel. Each spatial channel indicates the presence or absence of a specific facies using values of 1 and -1, which has the result that the facies represantatino of each facies looks like [-1,1,-1] given that FA2 was present in that sepcific voxel.

\section{Fluvgan Framework}\label{Subsec:Fluvgan}
\subsection{Model Architecture}\label{Subsec:Model_architecture}
The GAN architecture used in this project is based on the \gls{fluvgan} model, introduced by~\textcite{rongier2025afluvgan}. Thie architecture adapts the BigGAN framework~\parencite{brock2018large} to manage the complex connectivity and high resolution necessary for realistic reservoir models. Where \textcite{rongier2025atowards} used a 2-channel approach for their model, one for grain size and one for deposition time, the model used in this project comprises of either 3 or 9 channels, where each channel represents another facies based on the one-hot encoding strategy. 

The dynamic channel depth is accommodated by an architecture comprising a Generator ($\mathbf{G}$) and a Discriminator ($\mathbf{D}$) built with 3D Residual blocks. These blocks enable the generator to transform a, in this case, 100-dimensional latent vector $\mathbf{z*}$ into a $C \times 32 \times 128 \times 128$ volume. This volume begins as a $512 \times 4 \times 4 \times 4$ feature map, created via a 3D transposed convolution, and is then expanded by five residual upscaling blocks. The final spatial resolution is processed through a Tanh activation, ensuring the $C$ facies channels are scaled scaled from -1 to 1. Finally, an argmax function decides scales the one hot encoded tensor to integers, ensuring that a 1 represents the facies inhabiting the voxel. Every generated voxel is then evaluated by the discriminator, acting as a binary classifier to reduce the $C \times 32 \times 128 \times 128$ input to a single scalar logit. This logit is produced through five residual downscaling blocks that iteratively reduce spatial resolution while increasing the channel count to 512. This 512-channel discriminator, alongside the generator, achieves training sstability by utilizing spectral normalization and LeakyReLU activations. Training stability is essential for managing the model's computational scale, which involves roughly 11.6 million trainable parameters for the generator and 2.0 million for the discriminator.

Evaluating the generated realizations begins by analyzing the foundational mathematical metrics, specifically Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE). BCE is deployed because the generator processes one-hot encoded facies, effectively turning the spatial generation into a~\textit{multi-channel binary classification task}. This classification task relies on BCE to penalize pixel-wise deviations between the generated probabilities ($\hat{y}_i$) and the true categorical labels ($y_i$), defined mathematically across $N$ voxels as:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{BCE} = - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left[ y_i \log(\hat{y}_i) + (1 - y_i) \log(1 - \hat{y}_i) \right] $$

During adversarial training, instability often manifests as exploding logarithmic losses when either the Generator $\mathbf{G}$ or Discriminator $\mathbf{D}$ becomes too confident in its predictions. Furthermore, poorly calibrated networks are highly susceptible to severe mode collapse, where the generator's stochastic capability is restricted. To investigate the optimal model architecture that yields stable training and diverse geological outputs, a systematic hyperparameter tuning study was conducted.

\subsection{Optimization \& Inversion}

While the 9-facies models are utilized to evaluate unconstrained generative performance, the 3-facies models are deployed to test practical conditioning against the \textbf{DEL-GT-01} well data. To successfully condition this 3-facies generative model to real-world observations, the overall inversion workflow relies on two primary mechanisms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Latent Space Optimization:} An iterative search to find the optimal random noise vector $\mathbf{z}^*$ that best approximates the well data.
    \item \textbf{Pivotal Tuning:} A targeted fine-tuning of the generator's mathematical weights to perfectly lock the generated facies to the hard conditioning data.
\end{itemize}

This two-step inversion process begins with latent space optimization. Latent space optimization freezes the internal weights of the pre-trained generator and utilizes gradient descent to iteratively search for an optimal latent vector $\mathbf{z}^*$. This optimal vector is found by minimizing a categorical cross-entropy loss between the generated facies at the exact spatial coordinates of the well and the true geological observations. These geological observations anchor the optimization, mathematically defined as:

$$ \mathbf{z}^* = \arg\min_{\mathbf{z}} \mathcal{L}(G(\mathbf{z})_{\text{well}}, \mathbf{y}_{\text{well}}) $$

This minimization yields an optimized vector $\mathbf{z}^*$ that often provides a close structural approximation, but frequently fails to achieve a perfect 100\% match due to the inherent mathematical boundaries of the learned latent space. These latent space boundaries are overcome by transitioning to the second optimization phase, known as Pivotal Tuning~\parencite{roich2022pivotal}. Pivotal Tuning uses $\mathbf{z}^*$ as a static anchor, temporarily unfreezing the generator's internal weights $\theta$ to make microscopic adjustments. These weight adjustments forcefully push the localized output to match the exact well data, while a strict regularization term prevents the collapse of the broader geological realism, expressed as:

$$ \theta^* = \arg\min_{\theta} \mathcal{L}(G_\theta(\mathbf{z}^*)_{\text{well}}, \mathbf{y}_{\text{well}}) + \lambda ||\theta - \theta_0|| $$


\section{Evaluation Metrics}\label{Sec:eval}
Evaluating the performance of generative models on 3D spatial grids presents significant challenges, as standard metrics like Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) or direct voxel-by-voxel assessments fail on unconditioned models. Therefore, we have to revert to evaluation metrics that are able to capture the larger complexity in an ensembe of samples and subsets of voxels.

The Wasserstein distance, also conceptually termed the Earth Mover's Distance, is utilized as a robust global validation metric to evaluate how closely the generated realizations resemble the training data. Highlighted in this context by \textcite{rongier2025atowards} to inspect a model's ability to learn complex geological 3D structures, this metric quantifies the optimal transport cost or minimum total effort required to reconfigure the generated probability distribution $P_g$ into the reference real data distribution $P_r$. Mathematically, the fundamental Wasserstein distance is defined as \parencite{panaretos2019statistical}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wasserstein}
    \mathcal{W}(P_r, P_g) = \inf_{\gamma \sim \Pi(P_r, P_g)} \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim \gamma} [\|x - y\|]
\end{equation}
By evaluating the optimal transport plan $\gamma$ over the set of all joint distributions $\Pi(P_r, P_g)$, this formulation allows the network to assess global morphological similarity rather than rigid pixel alignment.Consequently, it effectively rewards the replication of realistic, non-linear geological structures even when exact spatial coordinate overlap between the training samples and the synthetic outputs is minimal.

While the standard Wasserstein metric provides an ideal mathematical framework for capturing global geological structures, computing it directly over large 3D grid volumes introduces a severe computational bottleneck due to the curse of dimensionality.To circumvent this operational constraint, the evaluation workflow specifically implements the Mean Sliced Wasserstein Distance (MSWD).This approach decomposes the high-dimensional 3D facies volumes by mathematically projecting their categorical spatial distributions onto a massive ensemble of lower-dimensional, 1D linear slices.By solving the optimal transport problem along these localized 1D projections---where closed-form solutions are computationally trivial---and averaging the results across multiple random angles, MSWD provides a highly efficient yet mathematically rigorous distance profile.Ultimately, tracking this metric across training iterations serves as a primary indicator of global architectural consistency, validating whether the newly generated geomodels accurately replicate the macro-scale channel connectivity and structural heterogeneity of the original training data while exposing early signs of diversity loss or mode collapse. 

Beyond evaluating the structural realism of a single generated model, it is critical to quantify the spatial uncertainty across the entire generated ensemble. This ensemble uncertainty is quantified using entropy metrics, beginning with Normalized Shannon's Entropy~\parencite{shannon1948mathematical}. Normalized Shannon's Entropy evaluates the pixel-wise entropy across multiple models, scaling the standard entropy equation by the number of classes ($k$) to strictly bound the metric:

$$ H_{norm}(X) = -\frac{1}{\log_2(k)} \sum_{i=1}^{k}P(x_i)\log_2 P(x_i) $$

This $[0, 1]$ bounding scale allows to visually map exact regions of spatial uncertainty, highlighting areas where the ensemble highly agrees versus areas of deep geological conflict. This 3D grid of spatial conflict is ultimately aggregated into a single definitive scalar using the Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence \parencite{lin1991divergence}. JS divergence measures the pixel-wise distributional disagreement and stochastic spread between the probability distributions of the flumy and \gls{gan} models, acting as a final quantitative measure of spatial fidelity:

\[ D_{JS}(P \parallel Q) = \frac{1}{2} D_{KL}(P \parallel M) + \frac{1}{2} D_{KL}(Q \parallel M) \]

where $P$ and $Q$ represent the categorical probability distributions of the flumy and \gls{gan} models respectively, $M = \frac{1}{2}(P + Q)$ and $D_{KL}$ represents the standard Kullback-Leibler divergence~\parencite{ kullback1951information}.

\subsubsection{Data conditioning}
While JS divergence defines the overall ensemble spread, after optimization and inversion, each individual \gls{gan} model within that ensemble must still strictly honor the physical reality at the wellbore. This physical reality at the wellbore acts as a strict spatial constraint, the localized mismatch of which is calculated using the Macro F1-score. The Macro F1-score calculates the unweighted average of the harmonic mean of precision and recall across all $C$ individual facies classes:

\[ F1_{macro} = \frac{1}{C} \sum_{c=1}^{C} \frac{2 \cdot \text{Precision}_c \cdot \text{Recall}_c}{\text{Precision}_c + \text{Recall}_c} \]

Where for each class the precision and recall scores have to calculated:

\[Precision = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}\]
\[Recall = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}\]

where true positive (TP) represents the number of correctly predicted positive pixels, false positive (FP) the
predicted positive but actually negative pixels, false negative (FN) the predicted negative but actually positive pixels.

Evaluating every facies class independently provides a highly robust performance metric that heavily penalizes the model if it ignores underrepresented minority classes. These underrepresented minority classes, such as the thin crevasse splays (FA2), would otherwise be completely overshadowed by the massive channel sand deposits (FA1) if standard statistical accuracy was deployed to assess the well conditioning.
