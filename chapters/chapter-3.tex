\chapter{Methodology}\label{Ch:Methodology}
This chapter describes the project workflow, beginning with the generation of 3D synthetic datasets used for training, validating, and testing the models (Section~\ref{sec:data_gen}). Section~\ref{Subsec:Fluvgan} details the \gls{gan} framework, including the network architectures, the facies representation strategy, and the loss functions employed during training. Finally, Section~\ref{Sec:eval} introduces the metrics and simulation methods used to evaluate the geological realism and practical utility of the generated realisations.

The primary real-world constraints for this project are derived from the physical well logs of the DEL-GT-01 and DEL-GT-02-S2 geothermal wells.~\textcite{bruining2023sedimentological} interpreted the cored sections of these wells, translating the raw physical logs into distinct, combined facies. The facies along the wellbore are presented in Figure~\ref{fig:appendix_flumy_facies_and_grainsizes}. The specific categorical nature of this well data serves as the link between the synthetic generative models and practical field application, driving two key methodological decisions in this project:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Because facies classifications denote discrete geological environments rather than continuous variables, they lack any numerical relationship (e.g., an integer index of $2$ does not imply twice the magnitude or a higher value than an index of $1$). Treating them as raw scalar values would introduce numerical dependency, incorrectly forcing the~\gls{gan} to optimize for non-existent trends between distinct lithologies.
    \item The facies of these well-log interpretations, which group the complex subsurface into three primary associations, directly necessitate the subsequent grouping of the generated dataset(s) into upscaled 3-facies dataset(s).
\end{enumerate}
 By aligning the model's categorical resolution with the exact structure of the available well data, it ensures that the generative framework can be practically conditioned on and evaluated against actual physical subsurface evidence.

\section{Datasets}\label{sec:data_gen}
In order to test the applicability of \glspl{gan}, new datasets had to be generated since training \gls{dl} algorithms for 3D geomodelling requires a substantial volume of high-fidelity training data. The new datasets were generated using FLUMY~\parencite{martin-hal-01313232}, a process-based stochastic modelling software designed to reproduce meandering channelized reservoirs. Based on sedimentological rules, the software reproduces facies type, grain size and deposition time for each voxel in the 3D volume.

\subsection{Sample Preprocessing}
To assess the capabilities of \glspl{gan} for the Delft Geothermal Reservoir, the synthetic training datasets are prepared at two distinct categorical resolutions. These two distinct resolutions consist of a high-fidelity 9-facies dataset and an upscaled 3-facies dataset. The high-fidelity 9-facies dataset preserves the detailed depositional environments generated by FLUMY, serving as a baseline to evaluate the model's pure generative capacity and its ability to replicate highly complex spatial distributions. While evaluating this generative capacity is crucial, practical field application requires conditioning the model to actual subsurface observations. These actual subsurface observations, derived from the cored section of the \textbf{DEL-GT-01} well, are strictly classified into three primary facies (Section~\ref{subsec:geology_DGR}). Because the hard well data only resolves to three distinct associations, the original FLUMY depositional environments must be grouped accordingly to create the upscaled 3-facies dataset. Beyond this grouping process, the categorical nature of both the 3-facies and 9-facies datasets requires a final preprocessing step known as one-hot encoding~\parencite{goodfellow-et-al-2016}. One-hot encoding is critical because standard neural networks process continuous numerical gradients and might otherwise incorrectly interpret integer facies labels as having a mathematical relationship. This misinterpretation is prevented by transforming the original 3D volume into an expanded 4D tensor, where each class is assigned its own dedicated spatial channel. Each spatial channel indicates the presence or absence of a specific facies using values of 1 and -1 (see Appendix~\ref{Appendix:One_Hot_Encoding}).

\section{Fluvial Generative Adversarial Network Framework}\label{Subsec:Fluvgan}
The \gls{gan} architecture used in this project builds upon the \gls{fluvgan} model introduced by~\textcite{rongier2025afluvgan}. Rather than a standard convolutional network, the architecture employs a 3D ResNet-based GAN design combining 3D convolutions with multi-scale residual blocks for stable gradient propagation. Where \textcite{rongier2025atowards} used a 2-channel approach for their model, one for grain size and one for deposition time, the model used in this project comprises of either 3 or 9 channels, where each channel represents another facies based on the one-hot encoding strategy. The deposition time channel was not used in this project.

\subsection{Model Architecture}\label{Subsec:Model_architecture}
The \gls{fluvgan} framework comprises a generator~($\mathbf{G}$) and a discriminator~($\mathbf{D}$) structured around deep 3D convolutional and residual blocks~\parencite{he2016deep, rongier2025afluvgan}. To capture intricate 3D fluvial geometries while maintaining stable gradient propagation, the network utilizes double residual blocks (\say{Double ResBlocks}) across multiple resolution scales. Rather than modelling continuous scalar properties or relying on a 2-channel formulation (e.g., grain size and deposition time), the network processes categorical facies representations using a multi-channel tensor of dimension $C \times 32 \times 128 \times 128$, where $C$ corresponds to the number of one-hot encoded facies channels ($C=3$ or $C=9$).

The generator maps a 512-dimensional latent noise vector $\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})$ into the target 3D reservoir grid through an initial 3D transposed convolution followed by five successive upscaling and residual block stages. The resulting multi-channel volume is passed through a $\tanh$ activation function, scaling the spatial channel activations to $[-1, 1]$, before an $\operatorname{argmax}$ operation discretizes the tensor into integer facies classifications. Conversely, the discriminator functions as a deep convolutional binary classifier, contracting the $C \times 32 \times 128 \times 128$ input volume through five progressive downscaling stages to output a single scalar logit representing sample authenticity. A macro-level visual overview of the generator and discriminator pipelines is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:fluvgan_architecture}. A complete, layer-by-layer architectural specification, including tensor dimensions and parameter counts for both networks (~21.05~million parameters for $\mathbf{G}$ and ~7.66~million parameters for $\mathbf{D}$), is detailed in Appendix~\ref{Appendix:FluvGAN_Architecture} (Tables~\ref{tab:best_generator_architecture} and~\ref{tab:best_discriminator_architecture}).

During the adversarial training process, the weights of both the Generator ($\mathbf{G}$) and the Discriminator ($\mathbf{D}$) are iteratively updated according to an objective function. The goal of this function is to quantify adversarial performance: measuring how accurately the discriminator distinguishes authentic geological data from synthetic samples, and conversely, how effectively the generator produces samples that fool the discriminator. Since the adversarial loss functions are purely based on the discriminator's outputs, which acts as a binary classifier (a sample is 'real' or fake), the appropriate loss function is the standard Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss. Whether the underlying data represents continuous properties or categorical one-hot encoded facies, the adversarial objective remains a binary classification problem at the discriminator level. Mathematically, evaluated across a batch of $N$ samples, the standard BCE loss is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{BCE} = - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left[ y_i \log(D(x_i)) + (1 - y_i) \log(1 - D(x_i)) \right]
\end{equation}

where $y_i$ represents the binary domain label (real or fake), and $D(x_i)$ is the probability assigned by the discriminator. 

During adversarial training, instability often manifests as exploding or saturating logarithmic losses. From an objective function perspective, these dynamics depend heavily on the discriminator's output; if the discriminator becomes completely confident in its classifications, the gradients provided to the generator can vanish. Severe instability or sudden drops in generator loss occur when the generator successfully exploits a specific artifact or shortcut that completely fools the discriminator, leading to a loss of gradient diversity. Such uncalibrated training dynamics make the framework highly susceptible to severe mode collapse~\parencite{prince2023understanding}, where the generator's capability is restricted, and it ceases to produce diverse geological outputs. Beyond the foundational loss formulation, the specific interplay of model architecture and optimization hyperparameters dictates the overall convergence. To evaluate the configurations that yield stable adversarial dynamics and realistic geological outputs, a hyperparameter tuning study was conducted.

\subsection{Model Inversion}
While the 9-facies models are utilized to evaluate unconstrained generative performance, the 3-facies models are deployed to test practical conditioning against the \textbf{DEL-GT-01} well data. To condition this generative model to real-world subsurface observations, the inversion workflow follows the two-step mechanism formulated by~\textcite{rongier2025btowards}: \gls{lso} followed by \gls{pv}~\parencite{roich2022pivotal}. 

First,~\gls{lso} searches for the optimal random noise vector $\mathbf{z}$ that best approximates the well data while keeping the pre-trained generator's weights frozen. This iterative search is driven by a composite loss function designed to satisfy two simultaneous objectives: honouring the hard conditioning well data and preserving global geological realism. The total optimization loss ($\mathcal{L}_{total}$) combines a context loss ($\mathcal{L}_{context}$) representing the well mismatch, and a prior loss ($\mathcal{L}_{prior}$) evaluated using the Discriminator's Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) metric:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{context} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{prior}
\end{equation}

Here, $\mathcal{L}_{context}$ explicitly penalizes deviations between the generator's output channels and the true one-hot encoded well observations, utilizing distance-based thresholding to localize the penalty. Simultaneously, $\mathcal{L}_{prior}$ ensures the generated sample remains mathematically indistinguishable from real data. By feeding the updated generation to the frozen discriminator and calculating the BCE loss, it prevents the latent vector from degrading into non-geological noise. In this specific implementation, the prior BCE loss is heavily weighted with a multiplier of $\lambda = 10.0$ to guarantee structural integrity during the gradient updates.

Because this latent space optimization often provides a close structural approximation but rarely a perfect voxel-by-voxel match,~\gls{pv} provides a second phase to resolve this. This second phase uses the optimized noise vector as a static anchor and temporarily unfreezes the generator's internal weights to make microscopic adjustments. This forcefully aligns the localized output with the exact well data, while regularization prevents the collapse of broader geological realism.~(A detailed mathematical formulation of this inversion process is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:optimization_math}).

\section{Evaluation Metrics}\label{Sec:eval}
Evaluating the performance of generative models on 3D spatial grids requires a balanced approach. Direct voxel-by-voxel assessments are strictly necessary to evaluate whether generated samples maintain stochastic diversity or if the model has broken down and is producing identical values across realizations (mode collapse). However, because unconditioned models generate novel, random realizations lacking a 1:1 ground truth, these rigid point-wise comparisons are intrinsically unsuitable for assessing true geological realism or structural coherence. Therefore, the broader evaluation framework relies on additional metrics that capture complex geological structures across an ensemble of generated samples.

To evaluate how closely the generated realisations resemble the training data's global morphological structures, we employ the \gls{msswd} (MSSWD). Building upon the optimal transport principles of the Wasserstein distance~\parencite{panaretos2019statistical}, which was highlighted for geological 3D structures by~\textcite{rongier2025atowards}, \gls{msswd} evaluates structural similarity without requiring rigid pixel alignment. Although projecting 3D volumetric data onto 1D linear slices inherently risks losing complex 3D topological connectivity, this dimensionality reduction is strictly necessary for our workflow. It leads to a significant decrease in computation time, enabling its feasible use as a rapid validation metric during the training process. By averaging the results over multiple downsampled resolution scales, MSSWD efficiently bypasses the computational bottleneck of high-dimensional grid volumes while capturing both local facies boundaries and macro-depositional geometry. It serves as the primary indicator of global architectural consistency and mode collapse during the training process. During training, the model saves 10\% of its training data exclusively for evaluation using the MSSWD metric, ensuring these validation samples are never presented to the Discriminator network.

Beyond evaluating global structural realism, quantifying the spatial uncertainty and stochastic diversity across the generated ensemble is critical. To achieve this, voxel-wise Shannon's Entropy~\parencite{shannon1948mathematical} is utilized. Let $N$ be the total number of realisations within an ensemble, and let $p_c(z, y, x)$ represent the empirical categorical probability of observing facies class $c \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ at a specific coordinate $(z, y, x)$, calculated as:
\begin{equation}
    p_c(z, y, x) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{I}(\text{Realisation}_i(z, y, x) == c)
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{I}$ is the indicator function. The raw uncertainty at each coordinate is computed via Shannon's entropy field:
\begin{equation}
    H_{\text{raw}}(z, y, x) = -\sum_{c=1}^{K} p_c(z, y, x) \log_2 p_c(z, y, x)
\end{equation}
To ensure the metric is bound strictly between 0 and 1, facilitating direct cross-comparison between models with varying numbers of geological classes (e.g., 3-class versus 9-class setups), the field is normalized by the absolute theoretical upper bound $H_{\text{max}} = \log_2(K)$, which occurs under a uniform distribution of pure randomness:
\begin{equation}
    H(z, y, x) = \frac{H_{\text{raw}}(z, y, x)}{\log_2(K)}
\end{equation}
By calculating the normalized entropy at each specific spatial coordinate across multiple generated models, this metric serves as a direct validation of the model's stochastic diversity. High entropy values at a given pixel indicate that the generator produces a varied distribution of facies at that location, whereas low entropy exposes areas of rigid uniformity or localized mode collapse. 

This voxel-wise evaluation visually maps regions of spatial conflict and agreement, which can be summarized across the entire volume domain to yield a global scalar score $\bar{H} = \frac{1}{V}\sum_{z,y,x} H(z,y,x)$. To evaluate how closely the spatial distribution of uncertainty matches the reference data, the coordinate-wise probability tensors are aggregated and compared using the \gls{jsd}~\parencite{lin1991divergence}. The \gls{jsd} measures the coordinate-wise distributional disagreement between the reference baseline and the \gls{gan} models (The full mathematical derivations and multi-scale details for these evaluation metrics are expanded in Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation_math}).

While JS divergence quantifies overall ensemble spread, after inversion each realization must honor the observed lithology along the wellbores. Because the reservoir is heavily dominated by channel and point bar sandstones (~67\% volumetric proportion), standard classification accuracy can be deceptive; a trivial model assigning all voxels to the dominant sand facies would achieve 67\% accuracy while failing to capture any minority facies. To prevent this bias, performance is primarily evaluated using the Macro F1-score, which gives equal weight to each facies class regardless of its volumetric dominance. Despite its utility in balancing class weights, a key limitation of the Macro F1-score is that it remains spatially blind. A false positive voxel immediately adjacent to the correct wellbore facies is penalized identically to one located far away, failing to account for physically plausible near-misses. The Macro F1-score calculates the unweighted average of the harmonic mean of precision and recall across all $C$ individual facies classes:

\[ F1_{macro} = \frac{1}{C} \sum_{c=1}^{C} \frac{2 \cdot \text{Precision}_c \cdot \text{Recall}_c}{\text{Precision}_c + \text{Recall}_c} \]

To evaluate this localized mismatch, the precision and recall for each specific facies class ($c$) are first defined as:

\[ \text{Precision}_c = \frac{TP_c}{TP_c + FP_c} \]

\[ \text{Recall}_c = \frac{TP_c}{TP_c + FN_c} \]

Here, a true positive ($TP_c$) represents the number of correctly predicted voxels for the target facies, a false positive ($FP_c$) represents voxels incorrectly assigned to the target facies, and a false negative ($FN_c$) represents actual target facies voxels that the model failed to predict. In addition to the class-specific F1-scores, the overall categorical match accuracy is computed to provide a high-level metric of the model's spatial reliability. Accuracy evaluates the total proportion of correctly predicted voxels across all classes relative to the total number of evaluated wellbore voxels, formally defined as the sum of all true positives divided by the total volume of conditioning points~\parencite{Google2025AccuracyPrecision}.
