\chapter{Introduction}
Sedimentary deposits are a vital commodity in modern society due to the wide range of resources that can be extracted from them. These resources include minerals, groundwater, geothermal heat, sand, and hydrocarbons~\parencite[e.g.,][]{morris2007genesis, donselaar2017relation, donselaar2015reservoir, brekke2001sedimentary}. Moreover, recent studies have investigated storage possibilities for carbon dioxide and hydrogen in such subsurface environments~\parencite{zhou2020dynamic, heinemann2021enabling}. Consequently, the demand for resources hosted within fluvial sedimentary deposits is expected to increase while the distribution of accessible reserves becomes sparser and more complex~\parencite[e.g.,][]{spittler2020modelling, calvo2016decreasing}. This highlights the need for a deeper understanding of subsurface environments to enable precise resource management, which can be achieved through geological surveys, conceptual evolution, and constructing high-quality 3D geomodels.

3D geomodels are traditionally constructed by engineers and geoscientists to predict reservoir properties and resource distribution~\parencite{song2022review, royer20153d}. The voxels (i.e.~cells) in these models may be populated by continuous~(i.e. grain size, porosity \& permeability values) or categorical properties~(i.e. facies). The predictions are fundamentally based on a geological conceptual model which acts as a hypothesis regarding the most likely rock mass configuration, built entirely from sparse field data~\parencite{koltermann1996heterogeneity}. Because physical data acquisition is exceptionally costly and time-consuming, engineers must rely on sparse field data. This unavoidable data sparsity drives the continuous need to improve geomodelling techniques. Such improvements aim to computationally bridge the gap between isolated data points and realistic, continuous geological representations.

\section{Problem Definition}
Despite ongoing advancements in data collection, data scarcity dictates that functional 3D geomodels rely heavily on subjective interpretations of the underlying geological conceptual model. Transitioning from these interpretive hypotheses into a digital framework requires applying specific mathematical and spatial algorithms. This introduces uncertainty at multiple stages, originating from both sparse data inputs and the modelling assumptions themselves~\parencite{hoyer2024evaluating}. As a result, precisely quantifying uncertainty is crucial for effective decision-making and can be accomplished using ensemble-based techniques~(e.g., \glspl{mcs}), which require generating a large collection of plausible 3D geomodels.

However, creating this extensive ensemble reveals a core drawback in conventional modelling techniques: the significant compromise between computational efficiency and geological precision. Figure~\ref{fig:object_vs_process_vs_pixel_models} illustrates the trade-off between generating geologically plausible samples versus the ability to condition to hard well data. Conventional modelling techniques exhibit distinct operational trade-offs: 1)~process- and rule-based methods excel at mimicking depositional physics but are computationally prohibitive to condition inversely to well data; 2)~pixel-based geostatistical methods readily honour hard well data but lack realistic geological connectivity; and 3)~object-based methods attempt a compromise by placing geometric bodies, yet struggle to replicate the intricate, non-linear architectural relationships present in natural sedimentary formations~\parencite{rongier2025atowards, sun2023geological}.

Because conventional methods force a compromise between geological realism and computational efficiency, a significant need exists for a modelling approach that can thoroughly incorporate complex geological prior knowledge while also ensuring the fast generation rates necessary for ensemble-based uncertainty quantification. On top of this, traditional modelling workflows are highly rigid; when our hypothesis of the geological conceptual model changes, the entire modelling pipeline must often be reconstructed and re-simulated from scratch. This inflexibility severely limits rapid hypothesis testing. 

To address the need for both geological realism, rapid ensemble generation, and adaptability, advanced \gls{ml} algorithms have emerged as a highly effective solution. Specifically, Deep Learning (\gls{dl}) algorithms can learn complex, non-linear spatial relationships directly from data, accurately capturing the geological complexity and non-stationarity that traditional methods often miss~\parencite{rongier2025atowards}. Furthermore, once fully trained and validated, the generative subset of \gls{dl} models can generate a vast ensemble of 3D realisations at a fraction of the time compared to slower process-based models, and can more easily adapt to changing conceptual priors. This rapid generation directly facilitates the large-scale uncertainty quantification demanded by modern subsurface engineering.

\section{Research Gap and Opportunity}
Recent advances in 3D \gls{dgm} have introduced various architectures for geomodelling, including \glspl{vae}, \glspl{ldm}, and \glspl{gan}~\parencite[e.g.,][]{laloy2017inversion, bhavsar2024stable, song2023gansim, federico2025three}. Although \glspl{ldm} currently lead in precise hard data conditioning, their iterative denoising process bottlenecks inference speed (ADD CITATION). \glspl{gan}, conversely, provide near-instantaneous sampling capabilities that are ideally suited for large-scale Monte Carlo uncertainty quantification. Despite known training challenges such as mode collapse, \glspl{gan} are selected for this study due to their computational efficiency and established history in spatial generation.

Training these models requires an extensive volume of high-quality data to act as the geological prior which prevents the model from generating samples that do not correspond with our hypothesis on the structural and geological rules of the subsurface. To overcome the natural scarcity of real subsurface observations, expensive but highly accurate process-based forward modelling software (such as FLUMY) is utilized as an engine to generate synthetic training datasets. Utilizing these synthetic datasets allows \glspl{gan} to effectively learn and cheaply emulate geological priors; however, a critical research gap remains regarding their ultimate deployment and conditioning.

While generating random, unconditional samples with a trained \gls{gan} is exceptionally fast, a significant algorithmic challenge arises when attempting to conditionally tie these models to real-world subsurface data. Standard \glspl{gan} operate directionally: they take random numerical noise and generate a 3D model. Therefore, \textit{inversion} is the mathematical process of working backwards—starting with hard well data and attempting to reverse-engineer the specific numerical noise needed to generate a model that matches those constraints. This process targets the model's \say{latent space,} which acts as its internal control board. However, in standard \glspl{gan}, this latent space is notoriously entangled. This means that features cannot be adjusted independently; forcing the model to tweak a specific local feature to match a well observation inadvertently distorts the surrounding geology, degrading or completely breaking the underlying geological realism it learned during training. Therefore, a central challenge in generative geomodelling is not merely generating visually realistic unconditional models, but maintaining that strict geological realism \textit{after} the model has been inverted to match hard well data constraints.

While \gls{dgm} excels in theoretical environments, recent literature emphasizes an urgent need to transition from idealized, synthetic test cases to practical field applications to truly test this inversion bottleneck. As~\textcite{rongier2025atowards} stated, \say{We now need to move towards more detailed studies on larger case studies closer to field applications to further assess how the whole workflow performs}. Similarly,~\textcite{federico2025three} noted that the application of the overall methodology to real cases should be performed. 

Consequently, the primary goal of this project is to bridge the divide between synthetic training environments and practical field operations by assessing the performance and invertibility of \gls{dgm} in a complex, real-world application. In practice, this means feeding real-world well data into a trained \gls{gan}, and evaluating whether the final output—a 3D voxel grid representing the reservoir—maintains realistic geological architectures while strictly honouring the data constraints. Testing these models in a real-world application requires targeting an active subsurface system, a role fulfilled in this study by utilizing data from the Delft Sandstone Member (\gls{dsst})—an active geothermal reservoir located in Delft, the Netherlands. This reservoir target is characterized using a parameterized fluvial conceptual model constrained by sparse well data. These well observations serve as hard conditioning constraints to evaluate how the generative model behaves under tight physical limitations. The resulting response in generative stability and uncertainty quantification is evaluated across multiple parameter configurations of the underlying process-based simulator. 

\section{Objective and Research Questions}
To bridge the gaps between synthetic theory and field application, this thesis poses the following central research question: 
\textit{To what extent can \glspl{gan} be used to create geologically plausible realisations of the \gls{dsst} reservoir while conditioned to sparse real-world data?}

To help answer this question, several sub-research questions have been formulated:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item How should the \gls{dsst}’s geology be parameterized in FLUMY to produce a training dataset sufficiently diverse for \gls{gan} modelling?
    \item Which combination of hyperparameters yields a stable training process?
    \item To what degree is the model able to reproduce the complexity inherent in the training data?
    \item To what degree can the model be successfully inverted to honour sparse well data without sacrificing the geological realism of the realisations?
    \item How does the choice of the underlying geological prior influence the success of the inversion process and the resulting uncertainty quantification?
\end{enumerate}
