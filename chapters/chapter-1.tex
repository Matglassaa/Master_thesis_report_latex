\chapter{Introduction}
Sedimentary deposits are a vital commodity in modern society due to the various resources that can be extracted from them. These resources include minerals, groundwater, geothermal heat, sands and hydrocarbons~\parencite[e.g.,][]{morris2007genesis, donselaar2017relation, donselaar2015reservoir, brekke2001sedimentary}. Moreover, recent studies have investigated storage possibilities for carbon dioxide and hydrogen in this subsurface environment~\parencite{zhou2020dynamic, heinemann2021enabling}. Beyond the increase in subsurface applications, the world also deals with an increasing population and energy demand. Consequently, the demand for resources hosted within fluvial sedimentary deposits is expected to increase while the distribution of the commodities themselves becomes sparser and of lower quality~\parencite[e.g.,][]{spittler2020modelling, calvo2016decreasing}. This highlights the need for a deeper understanding of subsurface environments to enable more precise resource management which can be achieved through various methods such as: data gathering surveys, geological evolution and constructing high-quality 3D geomodels.

3D geomodels are traditionally constructed by engineers and geoscientists to predict reservoir properties and resource distribution~\parencite{song2022review, royer20153d}. The voxels (i.e.~cells) in these models may be populated by continuous or categorical properties: grain size, porosity \& permeability values or facies. The predictions are fundamentally based on a geological conceptual model which acts as a hypothesis regarding the most likely rock mass configuration, built entirely from sparse field data~\parencite{koltermann1996heterogeneity}. Data sparsity is an unavoidable reality of subsurface exploration, resulting from the exceptionally high costs and extended runtimes required for physical data acquisition. Therefore, engineers develop a working understanding of the subsurface based on limited information. This reliance on limited information drives the continuous need to improve geomodelling techniques. Such improvements aim to computationally bridge the gap between isolated data points and realistic, continuous geological representations.

\section{Problem definition}
Despite ongoing advancements in data collection, data scarcity dictates that functional 3D geomodels must rely heavily on subjective interpretations of the underlying geological conceptual model. Transitioning from these interpretive hypotheses into a digital framework requires applying specific mathematical and spatial algorithms thus introducing uncertainty at multiple stages, stemming from both the sparse data inputs and the modelling steps themselves~\parencite{hoyer2024evaluating}. As a result, precisely quantifying uncertainty is crucial for effective decision-making and can be accomplished with various techniques~(e.g. \glspl{mcs}), generally asking for the creation of a large collection of probable 3D geomodels. However, creating this extensive ensemble reveals a core drawback in conventional modelling techniques: the significant compromise between computational efficiency and geological precision.

Figure~\ref{fig:object_vs_process_vs_pixel_models} illustrates the trade-off between generating geologic realistic samples versus the ability to condition to hard well data. Where process- and rule- based methods excel at mimicking depositional processes, their forward-simulating nature makes it hard to inversely fit them to well-data. Pixel-based methods on the other hand offer the contrary: being able to fit easily to hard well data but lacking geologic realism. Finally, object base methods were introduced as a \say{best of both world's} solution, placing geologic objects that fit the hard well data. However, due to the fact that these approaches are often limited to simple statistical distributions or strict object-oriented rules~\parencite{rongier2025atowards}, they find it challenging to replicate the intricate, non-linear architectural relationships present in natural sedimentary formations~\parencite{sun2023geological}.

 As a result, existing workflows compel engineers to decide between models that can quickly produce a high number of samples while adhering to well data but do possess limited geological realism, or geologically realistic models that require substantial effort to produce in large quantities~\parencite{ENEMARK2019310}. This bottleneck highlights a significant challenge in contemporary subsurface characterization. A significant need exists for a modelling approach that can thoroughly incorporate complex geological prior knowledge while also ensuring the fast generation rates necessary for ensemble-based uncertainty quantification. As a result, the question arises how the existing modelling process can be modified to ensure that:
\begin{itemize}
    \item 1) Previous geological insights and structural information is better employed.
    \item 2) Uncertainty in the produced models is quantified more precisely and efficiently.
\end{itemize}  
To address the need for both geological realism and rapid ensemble generation, recent research has proposed advanced \gls{ml} algorithms as a highly effective solution. These algorithms, specifically the modern subset known as \gls{dl}, possess the ability to learn complex, non-linear spatial relationships directly from data. By learning these intricate relationships, \gls{dl} can accurately capture the geological complexity and non-stationarity that traditional methods often miss~\parencite{rongier2025atowards}. Furthermore, once such a model is fully trained and validated, the generative subset of \gls{dl} models can generate a vast ensemble of 3D realisations at a fraction of the time compared to slower process-based models. This rapid generation directly facilitates the large-scale uncertainty quantification demanded by modern subsurface engineering.

\section{Research gap and opportunity}
Recent advances in 3D \gls{dgm} have introduced various architectures for geomodelling, including \glspl{vae}, \glspl{ldm}, and \glspl{gan}~\parencite[e.g.,][]{laloy2017inversion, bhavsar2024stable, song2023gansim, federico2025three}. Although \glspl{ldm} currently lead in precise hard data conditioning, their iterative denoising process inherently bottlenecks inference speed. \glspl{gan}, conversely, provide near-instantaneous sampling capabilities that are ideally suited for large-scale Monte Carlo uncertainty quantification. Despite known training challenges such as mode collapse, \glspl{gan} are selected for this study due to their computational efficiency and established history in spatial generation. Training these models, however, requires an extensive volume of high-quality data to act as the geological prior. To overcome the natural scarcity of real subsurface observations, process-based forward modelling is utilized to generate robust synthetic training datasets.

Utilizing these synthetic datasets allows \glspl{gan} to effectively learn geological priors; however, a critical research gap remains regarding their ultimate deployment. While \gls{dgm} excels in theoretical environments, recent literature emphasizes an urgent need to transition from idealized, synthetic test cases to practical field applications. As~\textcite{rongier2025atowards} stated, \say{We now need to move towards more detailed studies on larger case studies closer to field applications to further assess how the whole workflow performs}. Similarly,~\textcite{federico2025three} noted that the application (and extension as required) of the overall methodology to real cases should be performed. Addressing this explicit mandate forms the core opportunity of this research. Consequently, the primary goal of this project is to bridge the divide between synthetic training environments and practical field operations by assessing the performance of \gls{dgm} in a more complex, real-world application.

Testing these models in a real-world application requires targeting an active subsurface system, a role fulfilled in this study by utilizing data from the \gls{dsst} reservoir. This reservoir target must be characterized using a simple geological conceptual model combined with sparse well data. Such sparse well data will serve as the hard conditioning constraints to evaluate how the generative model behaves under tight physical limitations. These limitations, and the resulting fluctuations in uncertainty, will be analyzed by testing the workflow across multiple distinct geological priors. Investigating the interplay between varied priors and sparse conditioning constraints ultimately establishes the framework for the specific objectives of this study.

\section{Objective and research questions}
With the research gaps in mind, the main goal of this thesis will be to assess the applicability of using a \gls{gan} to generate realisations of a real-world reservoir by conditioning it to well data. Based on the outcome of this thesis, new insight can be given into the workflow needed to approach applying and evaluating \glspl{gan} for generating subsurface reservoir realisations. Hence, the main research question of this work is:\textit{To what extent can \glspl{gan} be used to create geologically plausible realisations of the \gls{dsst} \\ reservoir while conditioned to sparse real-world data?}
To help answer this question, several sub-research questions have been formulated:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item How should the \gls{dsst}’s geology be parameterized in FLUMY to produce a training dataset sufficiently diverse for \gls{dgm} modelling?
    \item Which combination of hyperparameters yields a stable training process?
    \item To what degree is the model able to reproduce the complexity inherent in the training data?
    \item To what degree does the model generate realisations that are successfully conditioned to well data? 
    \item How different do models trained on different geological priors react when sparse data is introduced?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis outline}
The remainder of this thesis is structured to systematically address these research objectives. Chapter~\ref{Ch:Background} establishes the theoretical foundation by reviewing the principles of geological modelling, the mechanics of deep generative models, and the specific geological setting of the \gls{dsst} Member. Building upon this theoretical background, Chapter~\ref{Ch:Methodology} outlines the core workflow, describing the generation of synthetic training datasets using FLUMY and detailing the specific \gls{gan} architecture deployed for the reservoir modelling tasks. The empirical outcomes of this workflow are presented in Chapter~\ref{Ch:Results}, which evaluates the training process and analyzes the structural and spatial realism of the generated realisations. These results are critically examined in Chapter~\ref{Ch:Discussion}, which contextualizes the findings, addresses the inherent limitations of the proposed approach, and highlights avenues for future research. Finally, Chapter~\ref{Ch:Conclusion} summarizes the main contributions of this thesis and provides definitive answers to the central research questions.
