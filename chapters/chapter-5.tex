\chapter{Discussion \& Recommendations}\label{Ch:Discussion}
The application of \glspl{gan} for 3D geomodelling offers significant computational advantages for large-scale ensemble generation~\parencite{sun2023geological,song2023gansim}. Previous works demonstrated that generating realistic 2D slices with a \gls{gan} is viable, while recent studies extended this to 3D volumes to condition model realizations to well data~\parencite{bhavsar2024stable,rongier2025atowards}. However, these existing studies relied exclusively on synthetic training and well data. To address this limitation, this work evaluates the workflow using a real-world case study of the Delft Geothermal Reservoir.

In this final chapter, the applied workflow is discussed: assessing the strengths as well as highlighting the weaknesses and consequently, providing the main recommendations in terms of model design. Finally, the recommendations regarding implementing the workflow for real-world purposes is provided.

\section{Interpretations of Key Results}\label{Sec:Interpretations}
The proposed~\gls{fluvgan} framework has demonstrated the capacity to replicate plausible 3D geological structures, while retaining sample diversity, given a balanced hyperparameter configuration. The presence of meandering and erosional features in the generated samples indicates that the 3D convolutions successfully capture dependencies across the entire volume, effectively learning both horizontal ($XY$) and vertical ($Z$) geological relationships. Additionally, when taking an ensemble of samples generated by the~\gls{fluvgan} model, the facies proportions are replicated almost perfectly. During the inversion process, the combination of latent space optimization and pivotal tuning proved successful in achieving mean Macro F1-scores ranging from 0.70 to 0.91 (with corresponding match accuracies between 0.78 and 0.92) across evaluated configurations along the well trajectories. More importantly, the region affected by the inversion process still shows good diversity in the generated geological structures with low influence on the overall ensemble entropy.

Inspecting the generated samples in Figure~\ref{fig:appendix_2D_Z_slices_models_5(1)_and_flumy} shows that the model is seemingly struggling with reproducing the training dataset with similar 'smoothness': areas with single isolated voxels of the clay deposits as well as continuous mudplugs at times being broken up by isolated voxels classified as sand body deposits, after which the mudplugs continue again. This suggests that the model is having a hard time with correctly reproducing structures with the same continuity as presented in the datasets.

Additionally, the global categorical facies distribution highlights a severe statistical imbalance that dictates the entire training dynamic. Reverting from the grouped three-class case—where the primary class dominates approximately 70\% of the domain and the secondary appears only 5\% of the time—to the high-fidelity case with nine distinct facies classes tests the ultimate operational limits of the conditional \gls{gan} framework. Managing nine simultaneous channels severely amplifies the structural complexity the network must resolve. The overwhelming volumetric presence of dominant sand bodies, such as~\textit{Point Bar} deposits, which occupy over 65\% of the grid volume across all settings, stands in stark contrast to minority facies like the~\textit{Crevasse Splay Core},~\textit{Crevasse Channel}, and~\textit{Crevasse Splay Delta}. These rare associations are suppressed down to occurrence rates between 0.0\% and 0.1\% due to fundamental boundary parameters. From a \gls{ml} perspective this creates a critical optimization bottleneck: because a standard \gls{gan} treats all voxels with equal weight, the discriminator is heavily incentivized to act as a shortcut detector, evaluating global sample quality predominantly on the dominant class. Consequently, while preliminary results from the high-fidelity model trained over 25 epochs indicate that large-scale structures maintain reasonable geological plausibility, the network struggles to accurately preserve minor facies. The low occurrence rates occasionally cause the model to find a stable loss minimum that completely discards these sparse elements. Crucially, the high-fidelity model struggles to achieve a good~\gls{msswd} metric on these barely occurring classes. This poor statistical convergence is a direct result of the severe class imbalance and low occurrence rate, which overwhelms the mathematical contribution of these minority voxels to the global discriminator loss.

To mitigate these challenges in the primary configuration, the applied hyperparameter ablation study proved essential for model stabilization. While base architectures suffered from mode collapse, achieving a normalized voxel-wise entropy score of just 0.515 compared to the baseline 0.69, stability was achieved by increasing network depth through double residual blocks, which preserved high-frequency spatial gradients across the $128 \times 128 \times 32$ volume. Regularization was identified as the primary lever for equilibrium, with R1 Regularization applied every 16 iterations achieving a normalized voxel-wise entropy score of 0.704. Furthermore, the study highlights that model capacity is tightly coupled with data quantity; reducing the training volume by half caused significant degradation in the~\gls{msswd} metric, confirming that higher-dimensional geological configurations require a sufficient dataset scale to ensure proper generalization. 

Although the~\gls{msswd} metric evolution provided no evidence of the class imbalance having an effect on the produced samples for the grouped classes, the connectivity analysis showed a clear mismatch in the blob size frequency of the two minority classes, Clay and Crevasse Splay \& Levee deposits, compared to the test dataset (Figure~\ref{fig:model_5_connectivity_distributions}). Moreover, well mismatch evaluation metrics (Figures~\ref{fig:post_optimization_f1_scores_distributions} and~\ref{fig:post_optimization_metrics_across_Z_axis}) show that the minority classes are less accurate when it comes to adhering to hard well data. Crucially, this analysis uncovered a decoupling between automated metrics and true geological plausibility. While the \gls{msswd} metric plateaued after approximately 2,000 iterations, qualitative analysis indicated that topological connectivity—particularly for minority facies—continued to improve significantly with longer training. This suggests that while traditional distance metrics are effective for monitoring statistical convergence, they are insufficient as standalone proxies for capturing the complex, long-range connectivity required in fluvial geomodelling.

In addition to the high-fidelity configuration, the evaluation of the multi-prior trained model revealed further generative limitations. Specifically, the multi-prior model failed to achieve good sample diversity, producing generated volumes with highly repetitive spatial configurations indicative of ensemble mode collapse. It is important to note, however, that no dedicated hyperparameter ablation study was performed on these multi-prior models. Given that stability in the base architecture was heavily reliant on rigorous tuning, it is highly probable that performing a similar hyperparameter study—adjusting network depth and regularization—could have mitigated this collapse and significantly improved the results.

Eventually, the following answers can be given to the sub-research questions defined earlier:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{How should the \gls{dsst}’s geology be parameterized in FLUMY to produce a training dataset sufficiently diverse for \gls{gan} modelling?} \\
    Parameterization requires calibrating the process-based simulator against sedimentological core observations of the~\gls{dsst}~(channel depths of 5--7~m, sand body extensions 80--120$\times$~channel depth, and a net-to-gross ratio of approximately~67\%). Establishing three discrete parameter suites~(Settings~1--3) spanning lower to upper deltaic plain conditions generates an initial baseline of spatial uncertainty~(spatial entropy ranging from~0.66 to~0.71). Crucially, the 13~simulated depositional facies must be upscaled into three primary lithological associations to mitigate severe volumetric class imbalance and match hard well classifications. However, deterministic boundary conditions within FLUMY~(such as centralized channel belts and fixed inlet avulsion points) impose an inherent horizontal non-stationarity that partially restricts unconstrained stochastic variance.
    
    \item \textbf{Which combination of hyperparameters yields a stable training process?} \\
    Adversarial stability is achieved by deploying a deep ResNet architecture featuring double 3D convolutional layers and double residual blocks to preserve high-frequency spatial gradients across the $128 \times 128 \times 32$~volume. Regularizing the discriminator with an~$R_1$ gradient penalty applied every~16 iterations provides optimal equilibrium, achieving a normalized spatial entropy of~0.704 and minimizing Jensen-Shannon Divergence. Furthermore, maintaining a 1:1 discriminator-to-generator update ratio and providing a sufficient training dataset volume~($\ge 20,000$~samples) are essential prerequisites; halving the training volume or altering update frequencies triggers severe mode collapse or structural degradation.
    
    \item \textbf{To what degree is the model able to reproduce the complexity inherent in the training data?} \\
    The optimized 3-class~\gls{gan} successfully replicates macro-scale volumetric proportions and captures long-range 3D spatial dependencies, including continuous meandering channels and channel-belt morphologies. However, the model struggles with micro-scale topological fidelity: it overestimates isolated single voxels for minority facies~(yielding a $\pm 30$\% excess in unique local patterns) and produces structural artifacts such as discontinuous mudplugs and closed channel loops. When subjected to extreme categorical complexity in the 9-class high-fidelity scenario, uniform-weight adversarial loss functions cause the network to completely discard suppressed minority facies~(occurrence $<0.1$\%), proving that multi-class categorical complexity requires non-uniform loss formulations.
    
    \item \textbf{To what degree can the model be successfully inverted to honor sparse well data without sacrificing the geological realism of the realizations?} \\
    The two-step inversion framework successfully achieves high conditioning fidelity along the DEL-GT-01 and DEL-GT-02-S2 wellbores~(mean Macro F1-scores up to~0.91 and categorical match accuracies between~0.78 and~0.92), but this adherence entails a distinct trade-off with localized geological realism. In~\gls{lso}, expanding the spatial constraint mask improves conditioning accuracy but carves out an anisotropic low-entropy halo and induces unnatural stratification around the well trajectory. In~\gls{pv}, unfreezing generator weights resolves the minority-class conditioning bottleneck but introduces localized over-fitting and non-geological artifacts near the wellbore. Thus, while macroscopic inter-well diversity is preserved, strict deterministic conditioning compromises local geological realism in the immediate vicinity of hard constraints.
    
    \item \textbf{How does the choice of the underlying geological prior influence the success of the inversion process and the resulting uncertainty quantification?} \\
    Under a single geological prior~(Setting~2), conditioning successfully anchors realizations to well data while retaining stochastic variance in unconstrained inter-well regions, though creating an anisotropic reduction in spatial entropy around wellbores. However, attempting to train a unified multi-prior model across combined parameter suites resulted in optimization instability and ensemble mode collapse under baseline hyperparameters. Consequently, cross-prior inversion could not be executed within a single model, demonstrating that capturing broader epistemic uncertainty across diverse depositional concepts requires independently tuned, prior-specific generative models rather than a shared parameter space.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations}\label{Sec:Limitations}
The process-based training datasets exhibit an extreme categorical disparity that introduces severe algorithmic challenges throughout the generative training phase. Dominant sand bodies, specifically Point Bar deposits, occupy over 65\% of the global grid volume, while vital minority architectural elements—such as the Crevasse Splay Core and Crevasse Channel—are volumetrically suppressed to occurrence rates below 0.1\%. Because the objective function of a standard \gls{gan} treats all geocellular voxels with uniform weight, the Discriminator is heavily incentivized to evaluate sample validity solely on the dominant class. Consequently, the Generator focuses almost exclusively on refining large-scale point bar structures, disregarding minority classes entirely. This statistical suppression is compounded by the framework's high susceptibility to ensemble mode collapse. While the model successfully mimics the macro-scale global volume fractions of the geological prior, spatial entropy metrics indicate a severe drop, revealing that the network collapses into a narrow subset of stable configurations rather than exploring the full stochastic variance of the fluvial-deltaic system. For subsurface engineering, producing an ensemble with such artificially deflated spatial variance severely underestimates the true geological uncertainty, risking overly optimistic hydrodynamic simulation profiles. Furthermore, the training dynamics themselves exhibit significant hyperparameter fragility. The stability of the conditional \gls{gan} framework was heavily reliant on the specific ablation study performed for the Delft Geothermal Reservoir dataset, implying that transitioning this workflow to a different depositional environment would necessitate a complete and computationally expensive retuning of the network depth and regularization limits.

When transitioning the framework to real-world subsurface applications, significant physical scaling and data integration constraints emerge. The fixed spatial discretization of $128 \times 128 \times 32$ voxels strictly restricts the simulated domain to a localized volume of $2560\text{ m} \times 2560\text{ m} \times 32\text{ m}$. While this resolution is mathematically sufficient to demonstrate a proof-of-concept inversion workflow between nearby wellbores, it cannot encapsulate regional-scale depositional sequences or extensive multi-scale heterogeneities without introducing severe truncation artifacts along the grid boundaries. A naive expansion of the 3D grid size to achieve full-field dimensions exponentially amplifies the memory footprint and training time, severely bottlenecking the current dual-GPU architecture. Additionally, the scope of the applied hard data conditioning presents a considerable operational limitation. The two-step inversion process—combining latent space optimization with pivotal tuning—proved successful for sparse, vertical well logs, but its efficacy when scaling to dense well networks, deviated and horizontal well trajectories, or integrating soft geophysical priors such as seismic data remains unproven within the scope of this study.

Evaluating the generated models also highlights a fundamental disconnect between standard automated \gls{ml} metrics and true geological plausibility. While automated distance metrics such as the \gls{msswd} are highly effective for monitoring macro-scale statistical convergence, they often fail to accurately capture the topological validity of the generated structures. During training, the \gls{msswd} metric was observed to plateau relatively early, suggesting optimal convergence, yet qualitative and connectivity analyses indicated that the long-range continuity of minority facies continued to improve significantly with extended training. This discrepancy demonstrates that uniform statistical loss formulations and standard evaluation metrics are insufficient as standalone proxies for the complex, long-range connectivity required in dynamic fluid flow modeling.

As an operational limitation, standard convolutional operations apply uniform spatial kernels across the grid (translation equivariance). Although the network successfully learns fixed boundary constraints (e.g., central channel-belt clustering and inlet avulsion points) from the training volumes, natural fluvial-deltaic environments often exhibit complex non-stationary trends—such as downstream fining or variable avulsion density—which may benefit from explicit spatial coordinate channels or conditioning trend maps in full-field applications.

\section{Recommendations for Future Work}\label{Sec:Recommendations}
To further advance the application of \gls{dgm} in real-world subsurface engineering, future research should address the architectural, mathematical, and physical limitations identified in this study. The most pressing recommendations are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Architectural Enhancements and Attention Mechanisms:} To eliminate topological artifacts (e.g., closed-loop channel artifacts and broken mudplugs), increasing network capacity via double residual blocks proved essential. Future implementations should explore 3D self-attention mechanisms~\parencite{brock2018large}, which capture long-range spatial dependencies across the entire volume domain beyond localized convolutional receptive fields.

    \item \textbf{Elimination of Topological Artifacts via Architectural Tuning:} As demonstrated, network depth, sufficient dataset scale, and rigorous hyperparameter combinations are essential for generative stability. Future work must investigate deeper or alternative convolutional architectures to fully resolve spatial continuity and eliminate the non-geological artifacts—such as closed-loop structures and broken mudplugs—observed in the current generated samples.
    
    \item \textbf{Transitioning to Continuous Grain Size Models:} To bypass the severe optimization bottlenecks caused by extreme categorical class imbalances, future workflows should evaluate modelling continuous properties (e.g., grain size or porosity) rather than discrete facies. Recent studies, such as those by \textcite{rongier2025atowards}, have shown highly promising results using continuous representations, which inherently smooth the mathematical gradients during adversarial training and may naturally preserve rare architectural elements.
    
    \item \textbf{Optimization of the Inversion Workflow:} While the two-step conditioning process successfully anchored realisations to hard well data, a dedicated hyperparameter ablation study focusing exclusively on the inversion phase is necessary. Systematically tuning the learning rates, the balance between well-mismatch loss and prior-preservation loss, and the pivotal tuning iterations could substantially improve the conditioning accuracy and reduce the frequency of non-honoring samples.
    
    \item \textbf{Mitigation of Class Imbalance via Advanced Inversion Loss Formulations:} If categorical facies modelling is retained, future iterations must abandon uniform voxel weighting during the data conditioning phase. Implementing a mathematically rigorous weighted Categorical Cross-Entropy (CCE) loss or a localized focal loss formulation for the context loss ($\mathcal{L}_{context}$) is strongly recommended. This will artificially amplify the gradients of vital, low-volume geobodies during latent space optimization and pivotal tuning, ensuring the inversion process does not strictly optimize for dominant classes while ignoring hard observations of minority facies at the wellbore.
    
    \item \textbf{Full-Field Upscaling and Spatial Scaling Constraints:} Research must expand beyond the strictly localized $128 \times 128 \times 32$ domain. To capture regional-scale depositional sequences without incurring exponential memory overhead, the workflow should transition to patch-based generation, progressive growing \glspl{gan}, or multi-scale generative architectures \parencite{federico2025three, song2023gansim}.
    
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Fluid Flow and Geothermal Simulation:} Geothermal reservoir realisations that achieve high conditioning accuracy should be subjected to multi-phase dynamic fluid flow modelling. It is critical to quantify how the \gls{ml}-generated topological artifacts and deflated spatial variance actually impact macroscopic reservoir behaviours, such as geothermal heat extraction rates and thermal breakthrough times, compared to traditional geostatistical baselines.
\end{itemize}
