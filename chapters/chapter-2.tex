\section{The Delft Geothermal Reservoir}\label{Sec:Delft_Reservoir}
The data utilized in this study is sourced from the Delft geothermal reservoir, situated beneath the \gls{tud} Campus in the Netherlands. 
As of February 2026, a geothermal doublet is actively producing heat from this reservoir~\parencite{beerda2026geothermie}. A doublet system consists of a two-well setup: a producer well extracts warm formation water, and an injector well returns the cooled water to the reservoir. At the Delft site, the producer well (\textbf{DEL-GT-01}) was drilled to a \gls{tvd} of approximately 2,337~m, while the injector well (\textbf{DEL-GT-02-S2}) reached a \gls{tvd} of approximately 2,100~m~\parencite{vardon2024endofwell}. The project serves both commercial and research objectives, acquiring extensive geophysical logs and core data, supplemented by surface and downhole monitoring instrumentation~\parencite{vardon2020geothermal, vardon2024endofwell}. Despite the high data density, the cored section is restricted to the first 80~m of the \textbf{DEL-GT-01} well (approximately one-fourth of the reservoir thickness), while \textbf{DEL-GT-02-S2} contains no cored sections. The primary target is the \gls{dsst}, a member of the Lower Cretaceous Nieuwerkerk Formation in the \gls{wnb}~\parencite{donselaar2015reservoir}.

\subsection{Geology of the Delft Reservoir}\label{subsec:geology_DGR}
The \gls{wnb} is a 60~km wide transtensional basin in the southwest of the Netherlands, characterized by NW-SE trending structural highs and depressions~\parencite{ziegler1990geological, donselaar2015reservoir}. The \gls{wnb} entered an active rifting stage during the Middle Jurassic~\parencite{bodenhausen1981habitat, den1996fluviomarine, willems2020geology}, leading to the formation of several half-grabens filled with terrestrial sediments sourced from neighboring regions~\parencite{den1996fluviomarine}. The progressive filling of these half-grabens coincided with a change in the depositional environment as a transgressive shoreline moved the system from fluvial to marine. The Nieuwerkerk formation comprises three members: the Alblasserdam, the \gls{dsst}, and the Rodenrijs Claystone. 

The Alblasserdam member was deposited in a fluvial environment characterized by stacked channels and floodplain deposits~\parencite{devault2002tectonostratigraphy}. 
The \gls{dsst} directly overlays the Alblasserdam and consists of thick, stacked fluvial-deltaic channel sandstones with high net-to-gross values, oriented NW-SE~\parencite{willems2020geology, mijnlieff2020introduction, bruining2023sedimentological, groenenberg2010targeting}. 
The \gls{dsst} is in turn overlain by the Rodenrijs Claystone, an organic-rich lagoonal deposit~\parencite{willems2020geology, devault2002tectonostratigraphy, bruining2023sedimentological}.

Based on the sedimentological analysis of the \textbf{DEL-GT-01} core and \textbf{DEL-GT-02-S2} sidewall samples by~\textcite{bruining2023sedimentological}, three primary facies have been identified:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{FA1: Fluvial and distributary channel deposits.} Primarily light grey, fine- to medium-grained sandstones featuring low-angle cross-bedding and ripple lamination, with unit thicknesses ranging from 5 to 80~m. 
    \item \textbf{FA2: Crevasse splays and levee deposits.} Composed of moderately sorted, very fine- to medium-grained sandstones interbedded with dark grey siltstones, with thicknesses ranging from 1 to 5~m. 
    \item \textbf{FA3: Overbank deposits and paleosols.} Consists of dark grey siltstones, claystones, and very fine-grained sand deposited in low-energy environments. This association is characterized by abundant organic material, slickensides, and siderite nodules.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:well_data} provides a visual overview of the facies identified in the \textbf{DEL-GT-01} and \textbf{DEL-GT-02-S2} wells, along with their respective proportions. Both wells exhibit a similar distribution of facies, with FA1 dominating the reservoir section, followed by FA2 and FA3.

The specific depositional environment of the \gls{dsst} remains a subject of scientific discussion. It has been attributed to both fluvial-meandering~\parencite{donselaar2015reservoir, vondrak2018reservoir, willems2020geology} and fluvio-deltaic systems~\parencite{van1993stratigraphic, weert2024multiple, bruining2023sedimentological}. Recent analysis by~\textcite{bruining2023sedimentological} suggests that the cored section represents a deltaic environment. The observed facies can be attributed to a upper to lower plain delta with multi-stacked fluvial/distributary channels, single channels, crevasse splays, levees and overbank fines.

\subsection{Deltaic Depositional Environment}
Deltaic environments form at the land-sea interface where river systems supply clastic sediments. Delta morphology is governed by the relative influence of fluvial, wave, and tidal processes~\parencite{kolb1966depositional}. 
The subaerial delta plain is typically divided into two domains~\parencite{kolb1966depositional, heldreich2017challenges}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Upper Delta Plain:} Lies above the limit of saltwater intrusion. Sedimentation is driven by distributary channel migration, overbank flooding, and crevassing. Environments include meandering and braided channels, lacustrine fills, and floodplains.
    \item \textbf{Lower Delta Plain:} Subject to river-marine interaction and tidal influence. Channels are more numerous and exhibit bifurcating or anastomosing patterns. Interdistributary bays, bay fills (crevasse splays), and marshes dominate the landscape.
\end{itemize}

In the \gls{dsst}, FA1 features organic material and siltstones indicating slackwater periods, while the lack of bioturbation suggests high sedimentation rates. 
The fine-grained nature of FA2 and FA3 reflects more distal positions on the delta plain. 
Siderite nodules in FA3 indicate anoxic, waterlogged conditions~\parencite{samuel1959some}, while soft-sediment deformation suggests rapid loading of mudstones over saturated sands. 
These features collectively indicate that FA3 represents deposition within a delta plain, likely in an interdistributary bay or a pro-delta environment.

\section{Geological Modelling}\label{Sec:Geomodelling}
The primary objective of 3D reservoir modelling is to provide a representative volume of the subsurface for fluid flow and heat transport simulation, aiding decision support. Ideally, these models are iteratively updated as new production and surveillance data become available~\parencite{emerick2025ensemble, ringrose2016reservoir}, although history matching and conditioning in complex heterogeneous formations remain challenging in practice. 
Existing techniques are generally categorized into three types~\parencite{koltermann1996heterogeneity} (Table~\ref{tab:Geomodelling_comparison}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pixel-based Models:} Use spatial statistics (e.g., variograms) for cell-by-cell property assignment. They are efficient and honour dense well data but often fail to capture complex connectivity, resulting in pixelated realisations.
    \item \textbf{Object-based Models:} Directly place geometric features (e.g., channels) based on geological rules. They offer high visual realism but struggle to honour dense or closely spaced well data.
    \item \textbf{Process-based Models:} Simulate the processes behind sediment transport and deposition. They provide the highest degree of realism but are computationally expensive and difficult to condition to specific hard data like well logs.
\end{itemize}

While traditional pixel-based geomodelling relies on two-point spatial statistics (e.g., variograms) to distribute properties, two-point covariance functions are mathematically limited in their ability to represent multi-point connectivity and sinuous channel shapes. Because subsurface field data is inherently sparse, statistical interpolation alone cannot reconstruct complex architectural bodies without an expressive geological prior. Advanced \gls{ml} and generative deep learning methods address this challenge by learning complex spatial priors directly from rich synthetic simulations (e.g., process-based models), enabling the generation of realistic architectural patterns that honour sparse conditioning data.

\section{Machine Learning}\label{Sec:ML} 
\gls{ml} involves the development of algorithms that learn patterns from data to perform specific tasks without explicit programming~\parencite{goodfellow-et-al-2016}. This foundational concept of learning without explicit programming has evolved significantly since~\textcite{samuel1959some} introduced the term, expanding into a wide array of computational techniques. These diverse techniques are fundamentally categorized based on their training approach into two primary domains:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Supervised Learning:} The algorithm is trained on a labeled dataset containing explicit input-output pairs.
\item \textbf{Unsupervised Learning:} The algorithm identifies hidden patterns within unlabeled data without prior knowledge of the desired output.
\end{itemize}
Within these two broad categories, algorithms are deployed to solve common tasks such as classification, regression, clustering, and generation. While \glspl{ann} and traditional \gls{ml} algorithms excel at analysing tabular petrophysical data, extracting spatial structures from high-dimensional 3D grids requires specialized representations~\parencite{schaaf2019quantification}. \gls{dl} addresses this by utilizing multi-layered neural networks that perform automated, hierarchical feature extraction~\parencite{goodfellow-et-al-2016, lecun2015deep}. For subsurface characterization, this capability allows models to learn intricate spatial representations directly from 3D simulations.

\subsection{Spatial Inductive Biases and Convolutional Networks}
Processing high-dimensional spatial volumes introduces a fundamental challenge known as the ``curse of dimensionality,'' where the data required to sample the space grows exponentially with grid resolution~\parencite{prince2023understanding}. To overcome this and ensure efficient generalization from finite training ensembles, deep learning models incorporate architectural constraints known as inductive biases. For spatially structured geological data, the premier architectural inductive bias is provided by the \gls{cnn}.

\glspl{cnn} leverage spatial inductive bias to become highly effective for 2D and 3D geomodelling tasks. Executing these spatial tasks relies on a core architectural innovation known as the convolutional layer. This convolutional layer replaces the fully connected neurons of a standard \gls{ann} with a localized set of learnable filters, commonly referred to as kernels. These multi-dimensional kernels operate by systematically sliding across the input data in discrete increments dictated by a predefined step size, or stride. This sliding stride is frequently combined with padding, a technique that adds artificial zero-value borders to the input to ensure the entire spatial region is comprehensively analyzed. Analysing this spatial region involves computing a local dot product between the kernel weights and the input values, a mathematical operation visually illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:2D_conv_example}.

In encoder-type networks (such as discriminators), the mathematical operation outputs a feature map where initial layers capture simple, localized edges, and deeper stacked layers combine these into high-level geological architectures, such as fluvial channels. Conversely, generator networks apply transposed convolutions and upsampling blocks to expand low-dimensional latent vectors into complete 3D spatial grids. Because the same convolutional kernel is translated across the entire grid, the network achieves translation equivariance, recognizing and generating consistent geological features across different positions within the reservoir domain.

\subsection{Geomodelling with Deep Generative Models}
In spatial geomodelling, generative architectures learn to approximate the high-dimensional probability distribution of training realisations, $p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})$, enabling the synthesis of new, previously unseen 3D realisations $\mathbf{x} \sim p_{\text{model}}(\mathbf{x})$~\parencite{goodfellow-et-al-2016, song2023gansim}. Prominent generative frameworks include \glspl{vae}, \glspl{gan}, and \glspl{ldm}~\parencite{prince2023understanding}. While each framework differs in its optimization objective, they all map between a lower-dimensional continuous latent space $\mathcal{Z}$ and the high-dimensional spatial domain $\mathcal{X}$. \Glspl{vae} explicitly regularize this latent distribution through probabilistic encoding; \glspl{ldm} generate samples via iterative reverse-time denoising; and \glspl{gan} learn implicit mapping functions through adversarial competition.

\subsection{Generative Adversarial Networks and Inversion}\label{Subsec:GAN_Theory}
\glspl{gan} achieve spatial generation through a competitive minimax game between two neural networks: a Generator ($\mathbf{G}$) and a Discriminator ($\mathbf{D}$)~\parencite{goodfellow2014generative}. The generator synthesizes spatial volumes from random latent vectors $\mathbf{z} \sim p_{\mathbf{z}}$, while the discriminator evaluates whether candidate volumes originate from authentic training data or the generator. This minimax optimization is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    \min_{\mathbf{G}} \max_{\mathbf{D}} V(\mathbf{D}, \mathbf{G}) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})}[\log \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x})] + \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z} \sim p_{\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{z})}[\log(1 - \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{z})))]
\end{equation}
In this equation, $\mathbf{x}$ represents the real training volume, and $\mathbf{z}$ represents the latent vector.

    \caption{Basic overview of a standard \glsentryshort{gan} architecture with a Generator, constructing fake samples from a random latent variable $z_j$ and a Discriminator deciding which of the two samples is the real sample~\parencite{prince2023understanding}.}
    \label{fig:GAN_architecture}
\end{figure}

The discriminator seeks to maximize this value function to correctly distinguish real from synthetic samples, while the generator seeks to minimize it to fool the discriminator (Figure~\ref{fig:GAN_architecture}). Through this adversarial competition, the generator learns to produce realistic 3D geological volumes without requiring hand-crafted spatial rules. 

While standard \glspl{gan} are unconstrained generative models, subsurface applications require conditioning the realisations to sparse hard observations, such as well logs. Rather than modifying the core adversarial training loop, conditioning can be achieved through post-hoc \textit{\gls{gan} inversion}~\parencite{bhavsar2024stable, rongier2025btowards}. Inversion freezes the trained generator weights and searches the continuous latent space $\mathcal{Z}$ for a specific vector $\mathbf{z}^*$ that reproduces the observed well constraints while preserving global geological realism. This mathematically decouples unconditional spatial training from localized conditioning, the detailed methodology of which is formulated in Chapter~\ref{Ch:Methodology}.
